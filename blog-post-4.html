Why Rural Education Needs Our Attention Right Now

RN
Rahima Noor
Education Faculty, Year 4
April 28, 2026
10 min read

I study education. I've spent the last four years learning about pedagogy, curriculum design, and child development. But nothing in my coursework prepared me for walking into a classroom with 28 students and a bookshelf holding fewer books than I own personally.

This essay is about that gap — what it looks like in the data, what it sounds like in the classroom, and why university students are, right now, in an unusually good position to help close it.

The Numbers Don't Lie

Turkey has made significant strides in primary school enrollment over the past two decades. Access is no longer the central problem. The problem is what students find when they get there.

  • Rural schools in Turkey receive, on average, significantly fewer supplementary materials per student than their urban counterparts. The gap is widest in the Eastern and Black Sea regions.
  • Teacher turnover in rural schools is disproportionately high. Many rural postings are filled by newly graduated teachers completing their mandatory rural service term — meaning the most experienced teachers tend to cluster in cities.
  • Library resources are often nonexistent or frozen in time. The school we visited in Ordu had an atlas from 2008. The countries listed on it have changed.

These aren't catastrophic failures of policy — they're the slow accumulation of neglect. The system works for the average student in the average city. It is failing students at the margins.

The Stories Behind the Numbers

During my faculty placement last semester, I spent three weeks at a primary school in a village outside Kastamonu. The teacher I worked with — Ayşe Hoca — had been there for eleven years. She was brilliant, committed, and visibly exhausted by the gap between what she wanted to provide and what she had.

She had printed worksheets at her own expense for years. She kept a small box of craft supplies under her desk that she'd bought herself from the local market. When I asked if the school received a supply budget each year, she laughed — not with bitterness, but with the tired familiarity of someone who had long since stopped expecting otherwise.

"These children are not less capable than children in Istanbul. They're just in the wrong postcode. That's the only difference. And it shouldn't matter."

— Ayşe Hoca, Primary School Teacher, Kastamonu Province

What Students Can Actually Do

Here's the thing about university students that often gets overlooked: we are a uniquely positioned demographic for this kind of work.

  • We have surplus resources. The average student has books they've finished, stationery they've accumulated, and notebooks that are only half-used. These are disposable to us and transformative to a school with nothing.
  • We have time and energy. A book drive, a delivery run, a school visit — these are achievable on a student schedule in ways they might not be for working adults.
  • We have skills. Design, logistics, web development, education training — the skills we're developing in our degrees are exactly what this kind of initiative needs to run well.
  • We have proximity. Many of us come from regions outside Istanbul. We have family in smaller cities, we know the geography, we understand the context in a way that outside organizations often don't.

None of this makes us saviors. The problems in rural education are structural, and they require structural solutions. But structural change takes time. In the meantime, a classroom with more books is better than a classroom with fewer. The children in that classroom can't wait for the policy cycle to catch up.

This is the space where student initiatives live. Not as a substitute for systemic reform, but as the thing that happens while we're waiting for it.
