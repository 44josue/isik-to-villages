Our First Village Visit: What We Found in Ordu

SJ
Shema Josue
Field Report
May 15, 2026
8 min read
Ordu, Turkey

We left campus at 5 a.m. Seven students, one university van, forty books, twelve pencil cases, and a collective case of nerves. Six hours later, we pulled into a village in Ordu province that most of us had never heard of before March, when our club was just a shared Google Doc and a lot of enthusiasm.

This is the story of what happened when Işık to Villages went from plan to reality — and what it felt like to hand a child their first brand-new storybook.

Getting There: Six Months of Preparation

The visit didn't happen overnight. We spent months coordinating with the local school administration, getting university approval for the trip, collecting and sorting donated materials, and raising enough funds to cover transportation. The logistics were humbling. Things we hadn't thought about: how to pack 40 books so they don't arrive bent. How to communicate with a school principal when your Turkish is academic and theirs is regional dialect.

We also had to be intentional about what we brought. Early on, we made a rule: no used books with torn pages or missing covers. Every item we delivered should feel like it was chosen specifically for that child. That rule meant turning down some donations — a difficult call when every book feels valuable — but it was the right one.

Arrival: What the School Looks Like

The school is a two-classroom building on the edge of the village. It serves 28 students across three grade levels, all taught by a single teacher named Mehmet Bey, who has worked there for eleven years. He greeted us at the gate, slightly skeptical — he'd had well-meaning outsiders visit before, people who came for photos and then didn't come back.

We spent an hour just talking before unpacking anything. He told us about the school's challenges: no school library, a limited budget for supplies, students who do their homework by phone flashlight when the power cuts out at night. He also told us about what the students were good at — particularly drawing and storytelling — and what books they'd most enjoy.

"The children here are clever. They just need materials that believe in them as much as I do."

— Mehmet Bey, Class Teacher, Ordu Province

The Distribution

We set the books out on desks before the students arrived. The team had sorted them by age range and subject the night before, with sticky notes matching books to grade levels based on what Mehmet Bey had told us. When the students came in, the room went quiet for a moment — and then it didn't.

A girl in the third grade, probably eight years old, picked up a picture book about the ocean and held it against her chest before she'd even read the title. A boy flipped through a science encyclopedia and immediately started reading aloud to a friend. One student asked, in a whisper, if he could keep the book or if we were taking it back.

We told him it was his. He read it on the bus ride home — we found out later from Mehmet Bey.

What We Learned

No amount of planning fully prepares you for the reality of the gap between what these communities have and what they need. But a few things became clear on the drive home:

  • Relationships matter more than resources. The trust Mehmet Bey slowly extended to us was worth more than any box of books. We need to go back — not once, but every semester.
  • Books are a starting point, not a solution. One distribution changes a classroom for a week. Sustained mentorship and regular supply replenishment changes it for a generation.
  • Students know what they need. Asking the children what subjects they liked, what they struggled with, what they wished they had more of — those answers should shape our next supply run.
  • Our team needs more language training. Half of us spoke limited Turkish and nearly all of us had zero experience with rural dialect. We're working on this before the next visit.

What's Next

We're already planning our second visit — this time to the Aydın school we've been in contact with since April. We're also setting a goal of returning to Ordu in September with a full classroom kit, including enough books to start a small library shelf.

If you want to help make the next visit possible, you can donate through our website or drop off supplies at our campus collection points. And if you're a student interested in joining the team for future field visits, we're recruiting. Come talk to us.

The boy on the bus read his book all the way home. That's why we do this.
